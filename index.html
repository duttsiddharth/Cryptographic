The deadline

An inventory by December 2027, and nothing starts until it is done

The Department of Science and Technology's task force, under the National Quantum Mission, published the timeline. A cryptographic bill of materials is recommended as mandatory in government procurement. The inventory is the deadline that bites, because identifying every place cryptography is used across an estate built over two decades takes months, not weeks.

Why now, not later

Harvest now, decrypt later

The usual objection is reasonable: quantum computers that can break encryption do not exist yet, so why act now? Because an adversary does not need one today. They need only to record your encrypted traffic today and store it until they have one. Anything with a long confidentiality life — customer records, medical files, loan documents, examination records — is being harvested on that assumption.

Mosca's inequality. If the years your data must stay confidential, plus the years your migration will take, exceed the years before a quantum computer can break today's encryption, then what you encrypt today is already exposed.

What actually turns up

The worst findings are rarely the quantum ones

Most organisations that run a quantum readiness assessment discover it was really an audit of cryptographic debt they already had. That is a good outcome, not a disappointing one — and it is a far easier budget conversation than a ten-year threat model.

Broken today

MD5, SHA-1, 3DES, weak RSA

Exploitable now, with nothing to do with quantum computing. These sort above everything else, because the risk is not hypothetical and the fix is not a research programme.

Broken by quantum

RSA, ECDSA, ECDH, Diffie-Hellman

Shor's algorithm breaks these outright. A larger key does not help. They have to be replaced rather than strengthened, which is why the inventory has to come first.

Weakened, or sound

AES, SHA-2, and the NIST standards

Grover's algorithm halves symmetric strength, so AES-128 moves to AES-256. AES-256, ChaCha20 and the post-quantum standards hold.

Honest limits

What it cannot do

Worth reading before you rely on it. A tool that oversells its coverage is worse than no tool, because it produces confidence rather than an inventory.

It reads text, not systemsSource code, configuration, certificates and keys. Not a COBOL core banking system, a mainframe, a vendor appliance, or anything compiled — and those estates are usually where the most serious findings sit. They need interviews and vendor attestations, not a scanner.
A clean result is the one to distrustIf a scan returns nothing, qsafe says so loudly rather than reporting a pass. An empty result almost always means the wrong path was given. Very few real systems contain no cryptography.
The forecast is an input, not a predictionNobody knows when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives. The default assumption of 2035 is neither the most aggressive nor the most conservative estimate in circulation, and you can change it.
It is the start of a review, not its conclusionTreat the report as the first inventory a human being then argues with. That is what it is good for.
